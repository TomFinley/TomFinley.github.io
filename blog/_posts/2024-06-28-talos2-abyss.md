title: "Talos 2: Into the Abyss Reactions"
---

Herein I reveal my extremely important observations of the Talos 2 DLC, which I
have just played.

1. **Unexpected Outcomes** This was quite the introduction to Abyss. The
   solution -- which involves what color reaches what through clever but
   interruptable signals -- then it becomes "easy." It definitely did have me
   scratching my head a good long while.
2. **The Other** If you are open to the idea of the colors passing through
   inaccessible connectors as something you can control, which was the lesson, I
   suppose, of the first puzzle, then this becomes easy.
3. **Oscillation** Took me a good few minutes, but once I realized the key was
   to connect the blue one to *both* the red and blue outputs it became clear.
4. **Alternation** This baffled me a good half hour, so I turned it off -- then
   I realized the true solution involving the "red" connector sometimes getting
   cancelled, to turn it on and off, and then the solution became clear. The
   sequence was, I quit in disgust, I thought about it for thirty seconds and
   somehow it occurred to me, and I'm back in the game.
5. **Tritogeneia** Fairly easy. What the arrangement had to be is obvious, then
   it was just a matter of getting the timings of raising/lowering gates right.
6. **Voyager** This took me about two hours, by far the longest of any of the
   puzzles, which is odd because in strict terms I think it's actually the
   simplest of the DLC. I got hung up on a strange inability to realize what the
   drillable wall between the two small rooms was for -- of course, it's used
   for something absolutely dead simple, but somehow despite actively meditating
   on that wall I couldn't make the connection for an embarrassing length of
   time.
7. **Stylite** Not too difficult, once I realize that there is *one* spot from
   where a single connector can connect the emitter to the final receiver.
8. **Metaphora** Easy once you realize that the key is to have the first "step"
   mechanism "beyond" the gate.
9. **Color Theory** This was fun in its way: you keep making your way through
   it, and at whiles you have to "leave something behind" to proceed, often
   swapping the "new" items for the more useful "old" items. It is however very
   prone to the problem where a stray misclick can completely ruin the sequence
   of opened gates, leaving you in an irrecoverable state and forcing a reset --
   which in turn discourages you from using the play-space itself to reason out
   a problem.

   There is a locked "two plate" audio-log that I was able to get, because I had
   two spare converters to just place on the plates. I wonder if there's another
   solution that doesn't involve getting the two plates?
10. **Metathesis** Cute. A sort of variant of Metaphora. I think I solved this
    in an unintentional way: I utlized the time between when a beam interruption
    actually interrupts to pick something up in a way I suspect the puzzle
    designers did not intend.
11. **The Ring** Adorable, and surprisingly straightforward, just involves a
    fair number of steps, and a wonderful mechanic of a spinning ring you can
    put mechanisms on. This feels very much like one of the best parts of Talos
    1, the gradual "accumulation" of resources, figuring out how to free
    elements that seem impossibly stuck, and so on, and the ring is a really
    lovely element. However, part of The Ring's charm is that its functionality,
    while *novel*, was *obvious*, which brings us to:
12. **Tidal Lock** This is not a good puzzle. Like The Ring it involves a unique
    mechanism of "spinning things," but in this case the entire puzzle is
    figuring out that mechanism. In contrast, in The Ring, this "spinning
    thing's" mechanics are obvious from the start, and it becomes a charming
    part of solving the problem.
13. **Here and There** Easy. Straightforward use of inverters.
14. **Reversion** Easy. I suppose the critical bit that gave me a minute's
    consideration was that the first "pressure plate" inverter was meant to
    acquire the color of the original inverter?
15. **Narrow Path** Easy, at least, relative to those puzzles that involve the
    use of the "swapper blocks." The conversion to converters then back into
    connectors is a little fun bit of progress.
16. **Fragile Balance** A shockingly simple solution, once you realize that the
    alignment of the two pillars with the emitter is not a *problem*, that the
    first beam is blocked is actually a critical element of the solution.
    Relatively easy, but still made me think more than the past couple. One
    thing that surprised me early on was that the two pillars were aligned "snap
    points" -- they look like general obstacles.
17. **Consequences** Delightful little ditty -- once you realized that the
    jammer could be used to "deactivate" one of the two connectors by connecting
    the "blue" connector to the red and blue emitters, the true solution becomes
    clear.
18. **Radiating Choice** Another in the theme of controlling flow through
    intersecting beams. It was obvious that the final state would have both the
    red and blue emitters to the "outside" connector", but I took, I think,
    rather too long figuring out that the "inside" connector needed to be in the
    second chamber to make this work.
19. **Trinal** A lengthy and elaborate "coliseum" puzzle that gives you solid
    hints of where you're making progress, and it's fairly clear even from
    pretty early on what your goal is. It just becomes a question of how to use
    the limited resources to accomplish that goal, and *when* precisely was the
    right time to strand one of the three "clones" up top. This might be my
    favorite.
20. **Interception** I must say I tire of these "conflicting beam mechanics"
    type puzzles. This particular one was one that hinged on establishing a
    totally needless connection, then intercepting that.
21. **Hierarchy** One of my troubles with the "conflicting beam mechanics"
    puzzles is that, as the way in which conflicting beams "resolve" themselves
    is something of a strange detail and quirk of the game, as opposed to a
    really core mechanic. In this case, I have to cross many beams and make
    beams to nowhere, and eventually I stumbled upon a "correct" configuration.
22. **Leviathan** Not too difficult, but entertaining. There is this moment
    where you think to yourself "is that it?" and then realize, oh no, no it
    isn't. I also appreciate the craft that went into this: in any other puzzle
    the formulation of a "you must direct the beam from this direction" would be
    accomplished through a simple iron cage, so that an entirely new mechanism
    was invented -- I can get behind that. I suspect some people might "miss"
    the little alcove, and so get frustrated.
23. **One-Way Link** Another beam interception puzzle. I did get the "right"
    solution, but if I were asked to explain why this particular arrangement of
    crossing beams yields the "correct" solution, I would struggle. The one
    thing I'll say for it is that for a few minutes it had me fooled in
    supposing that the "trapped" inverter was essential to the solution.
24. **Halls of Power** This is basically a repeat of puzzle #2, except with
    three inverters instead of two, and on a larger scale. Given that it was the
    last puzzle, I was expecting something more, somehow.

## General Themes

Alternating colors puzzles, I'd put 1, 2, 24 in that batch.

Connector cancellation puzzles are 3, 4, 5, 17, and 23.

Beam-crossing puzzles 18, 20, 21, 23 (though this last one uses connector
cancellation to stop the beam crossing).

Two-steps-forward-one-back involve heavy use of newly-found elements "further"
in the puzzle to maintain/change the relationship of already "established"
openings. E.g., in 9, one uses an inverter first to open the first gate, but
then using some connectors further on you can essentially swap a connector for
the inverter, thereby enabling further progress, etc., and basically the whole
puzzle is doing that again, and again, and again. 8, 9, 10, sort-of 11.

"Gathering Forces" is closely related to two-steps forward and has significant
overlap, but there's a general theme of acquiring "more stuff." I'd phrase 8, 9,
10, 11 (though this has a unique mechanism that essentially puts it in a
category all its own). We might also put your "classic" Talos puzzle that
involves gathering/moving elements step by step, with no particular theme
include 6, 7, 13, 14, 15, 19, 22.

BS mechanic is 12. I mean, really.

advantage of superior equipment and positioning to progress. 8, 9, 10, 11
